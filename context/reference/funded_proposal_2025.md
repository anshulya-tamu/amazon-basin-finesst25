	Introduction
The Amazon River Basin (hereafter referred to as the Amazon), the world's largest river basin, plays a crucial role in global water, energy, and carbon cycles owing to its complex hydrology, dense forests, and variable freshwater storage1–3. The Amazon's surface waters—including lakes, reservoirs, wetlands, and the river water surface area—cover about 20% of the total basin area 4. These aquatic environments are crucial for the hydrological cycle, biodiversity, and carbon and methane outgassing5–9. Unfortunately, in recent times, these waters have been increasingly affected by extreme droughts and floods at global and regional scales10, emphasizing the need for comprehensive monitoring of surface water dynamics to support effective water management and disaster preparedness. A major variable at the nexus of the water, energy, and carbon cycles is evapotranspiration (ET)11,12, which constitutes evaporation (from land and water surfaces) and transpiration (from vegetation). ET drives local and regional atmospheric moisture and temperatures, resulting in about a 25-50% precipitation recycling ratio in the region13–15. Therefore, monitoring the Amazon's ET becomes imperative for deciphering the hydrological cycle and improving water resource management practices16. 
While many studies have advanced our understanding of rainforest ET in the Amazon, open‐water river evaporation remains comparatively understudied. Existing basin‐scale ET products seldom isolate open‐water evaporation17–20, although a few regional studies incorporate open‐water physics for lakes or reservoirs21–23. Yet these approaches generally apply still‐water frameworks to river surfaces, overlooking flow‐driven processes. However, recent studies suggests that river evaporation may diverge from lake evaporation because of turbulent flow and in‐stream advection, implying the need for separate formulations24–27. Beyond these knowledge gaps, the river surface area itself is substantial at about 70,000 km², almost three times the lake/reservoir area across the Amazon (Figure 1a-c; estimated from existing datasets28,29). The open‐water evaporation is especially intensified during the period of July-November and/or whenever the region is under drought, which is characterized by low precipitation and heightened solar radiation (Figure 1d).

This proposal thus aims to characterize flow‐driven evaporation in Amazon rivers and understand how it varies seasonally and interannually, particularly under extreme conditions. Advanced remote sensing (such as Landsat, Sentinel, Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT), NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR)) provides a critical path forward, enabling large‐scale mapping of water surfaces, flow velocities, and thermal properties. Objective, framework, and scientific questions
Our objective is to quantify open‐water evaporation from the Amazon river network, compare it with still-water evaporation, and identify the key drivers shaping its seasonal‐to‐interannual variability, especially under extreme conditions. 
We will combine ERA5/ERA5‐Land meteorological data, hydrodynamic modeling, and multi‐satellite observations (SWOT, NISAR, Landsat, Sentinel) to produce monthly values for open water evaporation flux (E) and volumetric loss () at reach scale from 1985 to 2025 (Figure 2). Guided by this framework, our research centers on the following key scientific questions and hypotheses: 
Q1: How do open‐water evaporation rates in Amazon rivers compare with those in lakes and reservoirs, especially during droughts and/or heatwaves?
H1: We hypothesize that flow‐driven advection and minimal heat storage create systematically different river evaporation, particularly under extreme conditions. If supported, we expect to clearly see higher or lower river evaporation than from a lake‐evaporation model, along with a stronger response during droughts/heatwaves. If unsupported, this indicates that flow dynamics have limited impact, or that atmospheric/ morphological factors prevail, yielding little difference between river and still‐water evaporation.
Q2: How do meteorological drivers and morphological attributes affect Amazon river evaporation losses (), and what fraction of total discharge is lost via evaporation under extreme conditions?
H2: We hypothesize that large‐scale climate patterns (as captured by reanalysis data) dominate river‐evaporation variability, while morphological attributes (channel width, flow regime) also shape sub‐basin differences. Although evaporation may remain a small fraction of total discharge under normal conditions, it can become significant during drought or heatwaves. If confirmed, we expect to see stable trends and a clear spike in lost discharge when conditions are extreme. If refuted, this indicates that local feedback mechanisms (perhaps local rainfall shifts, or channel modifications) overshadow these broader controls, indicating further research is needed.
Q3: How does in‐stream advective heat flux influence the location of high‐evaporation “hotspots,” and how do these patterns change under normal and extreme conditions?
H3: We hypothesize that a higher advective heat flux (driven by greater discharge or elevated water temperature) intensifies evaporation, forming persistent “hotspots” that peak during heatwaves or low‐inflow events. If supported, we expect a strong correlation between advective flux and river evaporation with notable spikes under high flow velocity or temperature gradients. If not, this implies that atmospheric factors dominate, and that in‐stream heat transfer has limited effect, even under extremes.

Comparing DSAL‐based river evaporation with DLEM’s lake estimates will address Q1 and reveal whether flowing‐water environments exhibit systematically different evaporation patterns, especially during extreme hot/dry events. Task 2: Quantifying Volumetric Evaporation Loss and Identifying Drivers (Addressing Q2) 
In Task 2, we will combine the monthly DSAL‐based evaporation rates () from Task 1 with Landsat/Sentinel‐2 derived water surfaces to obtain basin‐wide volumetric losses () and will evaluate which key drivers control seasonal and interannual changes (Q2). First, we will construct monthly composites of surface‐water inundation (1985–2025) after an advanced algorithm which eliminates cloud contamination effects40. This will produce 30 m surface‐water classifications of channels and lakes/reservoirs (see Figure 5). We then will multiply these area maps by the DSAL‐based E to obtain monthly evaporation volume values (). To quantify how much of the river outflow is lost to evaporation, we will compare  with discharge  data from the hydrodynamic model and from in‐situ gauges, paying particular attention to droughts or heatwaves (periods identified in Task 1). This should reveal whether evaporation, which remains a minor fraction of discharge under normal conditions, becomes notable during extreme events. To detect long‐term spatiotemporal trends in , we will apply the Mann–Kendall test41 and the Theil–Sen slope quantification42 at both the reach and sub-basin scale, over the 41-year record and test for monotonic long‐term increases or decreases in evaporation volumes. We will then use wavelet decomposition43 to separate seasonal from interannual signals. If the wavelet power spectrum reveals dominant multi‐year periodicities, we can link these fluctuations to broader meteorological drivers like the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO); whereas a strong annual peak would indicate a stronger influence of local flood cycles (or short‐term meteorological forcing).

If AHF remains a strong predictor of E after accounting for atmospheric variables, this implies that in‐stream advection substantially enhances evaporation in certain reaches. This analysis identifies the river segments most vulnerable to water losses under warming or reduced inflows, guiding management efforts to mitigate increased hydrological stress.
Uncertainty Quantification and Contingency Planning
Since direct flux measurements over large Amazonian rivers are unavailable, we will rely on Monte Carlo ensembles by perturbing DSAL inputs (wind speed, humidity, velocity, roughness length) to generate probability distributions of evaporation (E) and volumetric loss (). This approach will ascertain the parameters (meteorology vs. velocity) driving the greatest uncertainty. We will also perform water‐balance checks in well‐gauged sub‐basins by comparing P – Q – ΔS to DSAL‐derived E and cross‐validate against the established DLEM model in reservoir settings. To address local river microclimatic biases missed by ERA5 we will run sensitivity tests on forcing inputs and do an error propagation analysis. Persistent cloud cover will be mitigated using cloud‐correction algorithms and, where possible, SAR data (Sentinel-1/NISAR) will be used to quantify systemic under detection of open water area. Despite this, we acknowledge that monthly compositing may smooth out short‐term events. Hence, a sub-monthly event-based analysis will be performed contingent on the availability of data.
Relevance to NASA’s priorities and SMD goals
Our research would contribute to the following Earth Science Division SMD science goals:
Water and Energy Cycle: The research will focus on estimating open water evaporation fluxes and their behavior during extreme events in the Amazon region. Doing so contributes to more effective water management, particularly in optimizing multi-reservoir operations, which is vital for sustaining water resources in this ecologically significant area.
Carbon Cycle and Ecosystems: Accurate estimates of surface water dynamics aid in quantifying greenhouse gas release from open water, which is vital for assessing its impact on the carbon cycle and fragile wetland ecosystems.
Societal Benefit: Ultimately, this research seeks to leverage Earth system science to inform decision-making processes and provide tangible benefits to society. 
Additionally, this project aligns with NASA's Decadal Survey44 objectives (H-1 and H-2), aiming to study the coupling of water and energy cycles and factors driving their short- and long-term changes. Specifically, it explores changes in open water evaporation and its spatiotemporal distribution under extreme events like droughts in the ecologically significant Amazon River basin.

Open Science and Data Management Plan
In alignment with the principles of open science and effective data management, our project "Quantification of Open-Water Evaporation from River Channels in the Amazon River Basin through the Integration of Remote Sensing and Modeling" commits to transparent and accessible sharing of research data, findings, and methodologies. This commitment is structured around the following key components:
Data Management Plan
Data Production: The project will generate multiple datasets relevant to quantifying open-water evaporation from river channels in the Amazon Basin. These datasets include:
Satellite Imagery: Raw and processed satellite imagery from missions such as Landsat, Sentinel, NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar (NISAR), Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT), Landsat and Sentinel-1/2 will be used. These data will be processed from Level-1 to Level-2, which provides geophysical variables at the native resolution, and further to Level-3, where variables are mapped onto uniform space-time grids. Additionally, classification images delineating surface water bodies—derived using advanced algorithms applied to Level-3 data—will be produced to capture the spatial and temporal variability of surface water dynamics.
Meteorological and Reanalysis Data: ERA5/ERA5-Land meteorological datasets will supply daily wind speed, temperature, and vapor pressure essential for DSAL evaporation flux calculations. These datasets will be cross-referenced with ERA5 skin-temperature data for estimating water temperature, ensuring consistency with satellite-derived observations.
Hydrodynamic Model Outputs: Model outputs from the MGB-IPH hydrodynamic model will provide river discharge, water levels, and flow velocities for the Amazon Basin. These outputs will serve as inputs for estimating the DSAL model’s  component and will be validated using available in-situ gauge data.
Evaporation and Volumetric Loss Data: DSAL-derived monthly evaporation fluxes (E) and the corresponding volumetric losses () will be computed by combining the evaporation rates with open-water extent maps generated from Landsat–Sentinel observations. These datasets will include time-series data documenting both evaporation rates and changes in water body extent.
Data formats: Satellite imagery will primarily be stored in GeoTIFF format. Processed data such as evaporation rates, flow velocities, and statistical outputs will be provided in NetCDF/CSV or similar tabular formats.
Metadata formats: All datasets will be accompanied by detailed metadata following ISO 19115 standards, documenting data collection parameters, processing steps, spatial and temporal resolution, and quality control measures.
Spatial Extent: The datasets cover the entire Amazon River Basin, including all major river channels and associated water bodies.
Temporal Extent: The project will analyze data spanning from 1985 to 2025.
Data Repository: Our shared data products will be archived, ideally by Earthdata (https://earthdata.nasa.gov) at NASA PO.DAAC, or alternatively, by another long-term NASA or Federal repository that SMD plans to provide to awarded projects without archival cost no later than 2025 (ROSES-2023). If these prioritized options are not available, we will use other open-source repositories such as Zenodo (https://zenodo.org) or Figshare (https://figshare.com) under a Creative Commons Attribution license. These open-source repositories charge no archival cost for a file that is uploaded to the public space and/or under a certain size limit.
Public Sharing Timeline: Data and metadata produced will be made publicly available no later than one year after collection, following validation and quality assurance processes to ensure data reliability and usability for the broader scientific community.
Software Management
In line with the anticipation that no new software development is expected, any incidental software developed will adhere to the Scientific Information Policy for the Science Mission Directorate, ensuring public availability to the extent legally permissible.
Publication Sharing
Our research activities are expected to yield two peer-reviewed manuscripts, technical reports, and conference materials. These publications will be made publicly available through open-access platforms and repositories, such as the NASA Technical Reports Server (NTRS) and suitable academic journals that support open access, to ensure wide dissemination and impact of our findings.
Other Open Science Activities
We plan to engage in additional open science activities, including hosting open scientific workshops and meetings, providing open science training to project personnel, and fostering the engagement of public communities in the scientific process from the onset of research activities. These efforts will be documented and shared through open channels to encourage participation and collaboration.
Roles and Responsibilities
Responsibility for the implementation of the data management plan and adherence to open science principles will rest with the principal investigator (PI) and the Future Investigator (FI). They will ensure compliance with all data sharing, software management, and publication sharing policies, supported by the project team.

		Mentoring Plan Agreement
The mentoring plan includes activities related to project progress, academic advising research development, career counseling, and professional networking. The mentoring plan will leverage resources and activities available at the university. The mentor and mentee agree on this plan. 
Mentoring Plan
Project progress
The PI will have weekly individual meetings with the FI to discuss progress related to the project. PI will offer suggestions when the FI runs into scientific and/or technical problems. As an expert in hydrological remote sensing, the PI will provide guidance to the FI on how to quantify the surface water evaporation in the Amazon from space. The PI will closely monitor the FI’s progress in completing the proposed tasks and provide suggestions to the FI for his fellowship. 
Academic advising
Before the start of every semester, the PI will advise the FI to make an academic plan. The PI will help the FI to select courses, both within the home departments and other departments. The FI will also be recommended to take the interdisciplinary seminar courses. The FI will have opportunities to develop interdisciplinary collaboration skills by taking this good range of courses. Meanwhile, the PI will help the FI to find a good balance between course work and research, such that research can maximumly benefit from what the university can offer.   
Research development
The FI will be an integral member of the PI’s research group, which includes postdoctoral, doctoral, and master’s researchers. The FI will not only work closely with the postdoc and doctoral students who work on satellite remote sensing, but also get to interact with the group members who work on hydrological modeling. The PI has published widely in high impact journals on developing novel algorithms and remote sensing datasets. In addition, the PI is also experienced in the field of hydrology and water resources. The PI will make sure that the FI will have the opportunities to develop research both in depth and breadth.
Career counseling 
The PI will meet with the FI regularly to discuss their career goals and provide feedback outside of the regular meetings as needed. The FI is required to conduct a self-assessment and develop an individual development plan (IDP). The PI will provide guidance in developing a plan to build up the required research, along with the personal and interpersonal skills to achieve the goal. The PI will have weekly individual meetings with the FI to discuss their progress related to the plan, as well as their strengths, weaknesses, and professional development activities. In addition to the guidance provided by the PI, the FI will be encouraged to discuss career options with other faculty they get to interact with.
Professional networking 
The FI will have opportunities to attend research seminars and professional conferences, and the PI will introduce the FI to other successful scholars in the field. The FI will be encouraged to be an active member of relevant academic networks, such as the American Geophysical Union (AGU), and American Meteorological Society (AMS). 
Opportunities at NASA
The FI will be encouraged to attend the summer internship programs at NASA that will help in giving a variety of exposure. Moreover, the FI will be encouraged to apply for post-doctoral opportunities at NASA, given their plans to build a solid career in the fields of research and academia.
Future Faculty Program
Given that the FI’s long-term career goal is to become a faculty member in a research university, the PI will recommend the FI to participate in programs by the graduate school (e.g., “future faculty”). The PI will volunteer as the FI’s faculty mentor. Through such program(s), the FI will be able to learn a full set of skills for applying for academic positions. 
Mentoring assessment
The success of the mentoring will be assessed by 1) peer-reviewed journal articles on high impact journals (e.g., Water Resources Research); 2) presentations at national/international conferences (e.g., AGU, AMS); 3) the progress of the FI toward completing the Ph.D. degree on time (e.g., passing the prelim exam); and 4) completion of training sessions at CTE.
